## What peer support is — and isn't
tags: peer support, role, peer specialist, lived experience, definition, scope
ask: How is a peer different from a counselor? | What should I say when someone asks me for clinical advice?
Peer support is a relationship between people who share lived experience of mental health challenges, substance use, or both. A peer specialist uses that experience on purpose to build hope, model recovery and help people find their own path. The relationship is mutual and non-hierarchical: you walk alongside someone rather than directing them.

Peer support is not therapy, case management, sponsorship or friendship. Peers do not diagnose, assess risk clinically, give medication advice or tell people what to do. When a question belongs to a clinician — a medication side effect, a diagnosis, a legal question — the peer response is to help the person put the question into words and connect them with the right professional, often by going with them. Being clear about the role protects both people and keeps the team working together.

## Core values of peer work
tags: values, mutuality, hope, self-determination, peer values, principles
ask: What does mutuality look like day to day? | How do I keep self-determination when I'm worried about someone?
Peer work rests on a few shared values. Hope: recovery is possible for everyone, and your presence is evidence of it. Mutuality: both people learn from each other; power is shared. Self-determination: the person chooses their goals, pathways and pace — even when you would choose differently. Strengths-based: you look first at what is working. Authenticity: you are real, honest and transparent about your role. Respect and dignity: people are experts on their own lives.

These values show up in small things — asking before offering advice, using the person's words, celebrating small wins, being reliable about follow-up, and telling people plainly what you will and won't share with the team. When you feel pulled to take over, return to self-determination: offer options, explore pros and cons, and let the person decide.

## SAMHSA core competencies for peer workers
tags: core competencies, SAMHSA, competencies, skills, peer certification, training
ask: Which competency should I strengthen first? | How do the competencies show up on an inpatient unit?
SAMHSA describes core competencies for peer workers in behavioral health, grouped into twelve areas: engaging peers in collaborative relationships; providing support; sharing lived experience; personalizing peer support; supporting recovery planning; linking to resources, services and supports; providing information about skills related to health, wellness and recovery; helping peers manage crises; valuing communication; supporting collaboration and teamwork; promoting leadership and advocacy; and promoting growth and development.

Most state peer certifications and the national credential build training and exams around these areas. A useful habit is to tag your session notes with the competencies you used — over a few weeks you will see your strengths and the areas you rarely practice, which makes great material for supervision and your continuing-education plan.

## SAMHSA's working definition of recovery
tags: recovery, definition, SAMHSA, health home purpose community, guiding principles
ask: What are the four dimensions of recovery? | How do I explain recovery to someone who feels hopeless?
SAMHSA defines recovery as a process of change through which individuals improve their health and wellness, live self-directed lives and strive to reach their full potential. It names four dimensions that support a life in recovery: health (managing conditions and making healthy choices), home (a stable and safe place to live), purpose (meaningful daily activities such as work, school, volunteering or caregiving), and community (relationships and social networks that provide support, friendship, love and hope).

SAMHSA also lists guiding principles: recovery emerges from hope; is person-driven; occurs via many pathways; is holistic; is supported by peers and allies; is supported through relationship and social networks; is culturally based and influenced; is supported by addressing trauma; involves individual, family and community strengths and responsibility; and is based on respect. These principles make a good checklist when you plan a session or review a goal.

## Recovery capital
tags: recovery capital, strengths, resources, assessment, recovery domains
ask: How do I rate recovery capital with someone? | What do I do with a low score in one area?
Recovery capital is the total of internal and external resources a person can draw on to start and sustain recovery. It is often described in four groups: personal capital (health, coping skills, hope, self-esteem), social capital (supportive relationships, recovery peers), community capital (access to services, housing, transportation, recovery-friendly spaces) and cultural capital (values, traditions and identity that support wellness).

Rating recovery capital together — even with a simple zero-to-five scale across life areas — turns a vague sense of "things are hard" into specific places to focus. High areas are strengths to build on; low areas become goals or referrals. Re-rate every few weeks so the person can see change. ## Purposeful sharing of lived experience
tags: self-disclosure, sharing your story, lived experience, purposeful sharing, storytelling
ask: How much of my story should I share? | When should I not share?
Sharing your experience is the peer specialist's most distinctive tool, and it works best when it is purposeful: brief, relevant and for the other person's benefit. Before sharing, ask yourself why — to instill hope, normalize an experience, build trust, or model a coping skill. Share the "how" of recovery more than the details of the hardest moments; graphic details of use, trauma or self-harm can trigger people and shift attention to you.

A simple shape is then–turning point–now: what it was like, what helped, what life is like today. Keep it short, then turn the focus back: "That's what helped me — what's been helpful for you?" Don't share when you're feeling raw, when it would make the person take care of you, or when it would compare struggles. Talk through your disclosure boundaries in supervision before you need them.

## Boundaries and dual relationships
tags: boundaries, dual relationships, ethics, friendship, gifts, social media
ask: Can I be friends with someone I support? | What if I know a participant from my own recovery community?
Boundaries keep peer relationships safe and helpful. Common ones: no romantic or sexual relationships; no lending or borrowing money; no gifts beyond what policy allows; no personal social-media connections while you're in a peer role; no sharing your personal phone unless your program allows it; and no doing for someone what they can do for themselves.

Dual relationships are common in peer work — you may meet participants at mutual-aid meetings, in your neighborhood or at work in another role. They aren't always avoidable, but they must be managed: be transparent, keep confidentiality, avoid exploiting the relationship, and bring it to supervision. If you hold another job at the same facility (for example in facilities or as a technician), be clear about which role you're in and never use one role's access to benefit the other.

## Confidentiality and its limits
tags: confidentiality, HIPAA, 42 CFR Part 2, privacy, release of information, duty to warn
ask: What can I tell the team? | What if someone asks me to keep a secret?
Peer specialists working in health settings are part of the treatment team and follow HIPAA. Substance use disorder treatment records may also be protected by the federal rule known as 42 CFR Part 2, which has stricter consent requirements for sharing. Never discuss participants outside work, in public places or on personal devices or social media, and share only what's needed within the team.

Be honest about limits from the first meeting: "Most of what you tell me stays between us and your care team. If I'm worried about your safety or someone else's, or about abuse of a child or vulnerable adult, I have to share that." Never promise to keep a secret before you know what it is. If someone tells you something you must report, tell them what you're going to do and stay with them through it when you can.

## Mandated reporting basics
tags: mandated reporter, abuse, neglect, reporting, child abuse, elder abuse
ask: Am I a mandated reporter? | What do I do if a participant discloses abuse?
Many states make peer specialists and behavioral health staff mandated reporters of suspected abuse or neglect of children, and often of elders and adults with disabilities. Your employer's policy and your state law define exactly who must report, what must be reported and how quickly. Facility policy usually also requires you to notify your supervisor or the charge nurse.

If a disclosure happens: stay calm, listen, don't interrogate or promise secrecy, thank the person for telling you, and explain what happens next. Document what was said in the person's own words and report promptly through the required channel. You don't need proof — reasonable suspicion is enough. Afterward, take care of yourself and debrief with your supervisor; these conversations are heavy.

## Motivational interviewing spirit and OARS
tags: motivational interviewing, OARS, open questions, affirmations, reflections, summaries, MI spirit
ask: What's a good open question to start with? | How do I make a reflection instead of asking another question?
Motivational interviewing (MI) is a collaborative conversation style for strengthening a person's own motivation for change. Its spirit is partnership, acceptance, compassion and evocation — drawing out the person's own reasons rather than installing yours.

The core skills are OARS. Open questions invite more than a yes or no ("What would you like to be different?"). Affirmations recognize real strengths and efforts ("You came back even after a hard week — that takes commitment."). Reflections repeat or rephrase what you heard, often with a guess at the meaning ("You're worried going home means going back to the same people."). Summaries pull several points together and check accuracy. A good target is two or more reflections for every question. MI fits peer work closely because it respects self-determination.

## Stages of change
tags: stages of change, transtheoretical model, readiness, precontemplation, contemplation, relapse
ask: How do I support someone who isn't ready to change? | What stage is someone in if they keep saying "I know, but…"?
The stages-of-change model describes readiness as a process: precontemplation (not yet considering change), contemplation (ambivalent — seeing pros and cons), preparation (planning and small steps), action (actively changing), and maintenance (sustaining change). Returning to old patterns can happen at any point and is part of many recovery stories, not a failure.

Match your support to the stage. In precontemplation, build the relationship, offer information without pressure and explore what the person values. In contemplation, explore ambivalence with MI. In preparation, help make a concrete plan. In action, problem-solve and celebrate. In maintenance, focus on wellness, relapse prevention and building a full life. Note your impression in your documentation as a peer observation, not a clinical assessment.

## Change talk, sustain talk and discord
tags: change talk, sustain talk, ambivalence, resistance, discord, rolling with resistance
ask: How do I respond when someone pushes back? | What does change talk sound like?
In MI, change talk is anything the person says in favor of change — desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment ("I want to get my kids back," "I could call my sister"). Sustain talk argues for staying the same ("Drinking is the only thing that helps me sleep"). Both are normal parts of ambivalence. Your job is to notice change talk and invite more of it with questions and reflections, without arguing against sustain talk.

Discord is friction in the relationship — the person feels pushed, judged or misunderstood. Don't push harder. Try a reflection ("It sounds like you're tired of people telling you what to do"), emphasize their choice ("It's completely up to you"), apologize if you got ahead of them, or shift focus. Arguing increases resistance; understanding lowers it.

## Wellness and crisis planning (WRAP-style)
tags: WRAP, wellness recovery action plan, wellness plan, crisis plan, triggers, early warning signs
ask: How do I introduce a wellness plan? | What goes in the crisis section?
A Wellness Recovery Action Plan (WRAP) is a self-designed plan developed by Mary Ellen Copeland and widely used in peer support; formal WRAP facilitation requires training through the Copeland Center. Similar personal wellness plans usually include a wellness toolbox (things that help), daily maintenance, triggers and responses, early warning signs, signs things are breaking down, a crisis plan (who to contact, what helps and doesn't, preferences for treatment), and a post-crisis plan.

The person writes the plan in their own words; you ask questions and offer examples, including from your own plan if appropriate. It's never finished — revisit it as things change. Encourage sharing it with chosen supporters and the treatment team, and keep a copy accessible. BlackLab includes a wellness and crisis plan form you can draft together.

## Strengths-based practice
tags: strengths, strengths-based, resilience, asset-based, affirmations
ask: How do I find strengths when someone only talks about problems? | How do I document strengths?
A strengths-based approach starts with what people have rather than what they lack: skills, interests, relationships, values, past successes, faith, humor and resilience. People who feel their strengths are seen are more likely to engage and to believe change is possible.

Ask about times things went better ("When was the last time you felt okay? What was different?"), about how they've survived hard times, and about what others appreciate in them. Reflect strengths you notice in the moment. In notes, write strengths as specifically as problems: "Participant identified their daughter and their faith community as reasons to stay sober and described walking daily as a coping skill." Goals built on strengths stick better than goals built on deficits.

## Trauma-informed peer support
tags: trauma-informed, trauma, ACEs, safety, choice, re-traumatization
ask: How do I avoid re-traumatizing someone? | What do I do if someone starts talking about trauma?
Most people in behavioral health settings have experienced trauma. Trauma-informed peer support follows SAMHSA's principles: safety, trustworthiness and transparency, peer support, collaboration and mutuality, empowerment, voice and choice, and attention to cultural, historical and gender issues. In practice that means asking "what happened to you?" rather than "what's wrong with you?"

Offer choices wherever you can (where to sit, whether to close the door, what to talk about). Explain what you're doing and why. Notice signs of distress — dissociation, agitation, shutting down — and slow down or shift to grounding. You don't need to hear trauma details to be helpful; supportive listening and connection to trauma-focused clinicians is the peer role. Watch your own reactions too; secondary trauma is real.

## Recovery-oriented, person-first language
tags: language, person-first, stigma, recovery language, word choice
ask: What words should I avoid in notes? | How do I talk about substance use without stigma?
Words shape how people see themselves and how others treat them. Person-first language puts the person before the condition: "a person living with schizophrenia," "a person who uses drugs," "a person in recovery." Avoid labels such as addict, junkie, abuser, schizophrenic, crazy, manipulative, non-compliant or "frequent flyer."

Recovery-oriented alternatives: "chose not to take the medication" or "declined" instead of "non-compliant"; "substance use" instead of "abuse"; "positive or negative test" instead of "clean or dirty"; "return to use" or "recurrence" alongside "relapse"; "seeking support" instead of "attention-seeking." Ask people which words they prefer for themselves. Modeling this language in team meetings and documentation is one way peers change systems.

## Shared decision-making and advocacy
tags: advocacy, shared decision making, self-advocacy, rights, treatment team
ask: How do I advocate without overstepping? | How do I help someone speak up in a treatment team meeting?
Peers support people to make informed choices and to have their voices heard. Shared decision-making means the person and their providers look at options together, with the person's values and preferences guiding the choice. A peer can help someone prepare questions, practice what they want to say, and attend meetings for support.

Self-advocacy comes first: help the person speak for themselves wherever possible. When you advocate directly, do it with their permission, on the issue they chose, through the right channels (the treatment team, a patient advocate, the grievance process). Be respectful with staff — you're part of the team and the relationship matters for next time. Know the rights process at your facility, and document advocacy as a peer intervention.

## Supporting someone in crisis as a peer
tags: crisis, peer crisis support, escalation, safety, staying present, crisis response
ask: What's my job when someone is in crisis? | What should I never do in a crisis as a peer?
In a crisis your job is to stay calm, stay present and connect the person to the help they need. Listen without judgment, speak slowly, validate feelings, and help the person feel less alone. Use grounding skills if they are open to them. Offer choices and avoid power struggles. If there's any risk to safety, bring in the clinical team right away — that's part of peer support, not a betrayal of it.

Don't promise outcomes you can't control, physically intervene unless you are trained and it's your role, or leave a person at risk alone. Afterward, help the person reflect on what led up to the crisis and what helped, and update their wellness or crisis plan. Then debrief with your supervisor; crisis moments affect helpers too.

## Suicide: warning signs and what a peer does
tags: suicide, suicide prevention, warning signs, ask directly, 988, gatekeeper
ask: Is it okay to ask someone directly about suicide? | What do I do right after someone tells me they're thinking about suicide?
Warning signs include talking about wanting to die or being a burden, looking for ways to end one's life, giving away possessions, saying goodbye, withdrawal, sudden calm after deep depression, increased substance use, rage or reckless behavior, and expressions of hopelessness or feeling trapped.

Asking directly — "Are you thinking about suicide?" — does not put the idea in someone's head; research shows it can bring relief. If the answer is yes, listen, stay with them, thank them for telling you, and connect them to clinical help immediately: in a facility, tell the nurse or clinician right away; in the community, involve your supervisor and use 988 or emergency services when danger is immediate. Risk assessment belongs to clinicians. Gatekeeper trainings such as QPR teach these steps in a short course.

## Harm reduction and many pathways
tags: harm reduction, pathways, naloxone, abstinence, multiple pathways, MAT
ask: How does harm reduction fit with recovery? | What if someone's pathway isn't one I used?
Recovery happens through many pathways: abstinence-based mutual aid, medication for addiction treatment, clinical treatment, faith-based programs, harm reduction, cultural healing or natural recovery without formal help. People often combine them. A peer supports the pathway the person chooses, even if it differs from their own.

Harm reduction meets people where they are and reduces risk: naloxone access, not using alone, safer-use supplies, fentanyl test strips where legal, and any positive change. It keeps people alive and connected, which makes future change possible. Know your program's policy, and be ready to share overdose-response basics: recognize the signs, call 911, give naloxone, provide rescue breathing if trained and place the person in the recovery position.

## Medication for addiction treatment and peers
tags: MAT, MOUD, buprenorphine, methadone, naltrexone, medication, stigma
ask: Is someone on buprenorphine "really" in recovery? | What do I say when someone asks if they should stop their medication?
Medications for opioid use disorder — methadone, buprenorphine and naltrexone — are evidence-based treatments that reduce overdose deaths and help people stay in recovery. Medications for alcohol use disorder include naltrexone, acamprosate and disulfiram. People using these medications are in recovery; questioning that is stigma.

Peers don't advise on starting, stopping or changing any medication. If someone has questions or concerns, help them write the questions down and talk with their prescriber, and offer to go with them. You can share information about local programs, insurance and pharmacies, and support people who meet stigma in mutual-aid groups by pointing them to medication-friendly meetings and communities.

## Mutual-aid and recovery communities
tags: mutual aid, AA, NA, SMART Recovery, Recovery Dharma, Al-Anon, meetings, sponsor
ask: How do I help someone find a meeting that fits? | What's the difference between a sponsor and a peer specialist?
Mutual-aid groups offer free, ongoing community support. Twelve-step fellowships such as Alcoholics Anonymous and Narcotics Anonymous use steps, sponsorship and meetings. SMART Recovery uses cognitive-behavioral tools and self-empowerment. Recovery Dharma draws on Buddhist practice; Celebrate Recovery is Christ-centered; Women for Sobriety, LifeRing and others exist too. Al-Anon, Alateen and Nar-Anon support families. Mental-health peer groups and clubhouses serve people with mental-health conditions.

Help people try several and find a fit — format, size, online or in-person, cultural identity, medication friendliness. A sponsor is a volunteer guide within a fellowship; a peer specialist is a trained, paid member of a care team with professional boundaries and documentation duties. Both can coexist in someone's life.

## Documentation for peer specialists
tags: documentation, notes, DAP, SOAP, BIRP, GIRP, peer notes, charting
ask: Which note format should I use? | How do I write about a session without sounding clinical?
Peer notes record what happened, what support you gave, how the person responded and what's next. Common formats: DAP (data, assessment, plan), SOAP (subjective, objective, assessment, plan), BIRP (behavior, intervention, response, plan) and GIRP (goal, intervention, response, plan). Use whatever your program requires.

Write promptly, objectively and respectfully. Use the person's own words in quotes for their goals and feelings. Describe peer interventions concretely (purposeful sharing, goal setting, resource linkage). Note strengths, not only problems. Avoid diagnoses and clinical judgments — your "assessment" section is your peer observations. Never include anything you wouldn't want the person to read; many people can request their records. Document safety concerns and who you reported them to, with the time.

## Working on a clinical team
tags: team, role clarity, integration, clinical team, collaboration, peer role drift
ask: What do I do when staff ask me to do tasks outside my role? | How do I share a peer perspective in treatment team?
Peers bring a perspective that clinical staff can't: lived experience and a relationship built on mutuality. Teams work best when everyone understands the peer role. Role drift — being pulled into tasks like enforcing rules, monitoring, transporting or clinical duties that undermine trust — is a common challenge. Clarify expectations with your supervisor and use your job description.

In treatment team meetings, offer the person's perspective (with their permission), their stated goals and strengths, and any barriers you see. Ask questions that center recovery ("What does the person want?"). Build relationships with nurses, techs and clinicians; small acts of reliability earn credibility. If you also work on the same unit in another role, be explicit with participants about which hat you're wearing.

## Self-care, compassion fatigue and vicarious trauma
tags: self-care, burnout, compassion fatigue, vicarious trauma, secondary trauma, wellness
ask: How do I know if I'm burning out? | How does a peer protect their own recovery at work?
Helping work brings compassion satisfaction — the good feeling of making a difference — and costs: burnout (exhaustion and frustration often tied to workload and systems), and secondary or vicarious trauma (absorbing others' trauma, with intrusive images, avoidance or changed worldview). Peers face an added risk: work can stir up their own history.

Protect your recovery deliberately: keep your own supports and practices, use supervision, notice triggers, take real breaks and time off, and have a transition ritual at the end of a shift. Signs to act on include dreading work, irritability, numbness, sleep changes, increased use of substances and pulling away from people. Talk to your supervisor or EAP early. ## Cultural humility
tags: cultural humility, culture, diversity, equity, identity, inclusion
ask: How is cultural humility different from cultural competence? | How do I ask about someone's culture respectfully?
Cultural humility is a lifelong practice of self-reflection about your own culture and biases, learning from the people you support about theirs, and addressing power imbalances. It goes beyond "competence" — you can't fully know another person's culture, but you can stay curious and respectful.

Ask open questions: "What's important for me to know about you and your background?" "How does your family or community think about mental health or recovery?" Respect language preferences, names and pronouns, faith practices and traditional healing. Recognize historical and systemic trauma, including racism and discrimination in health systems. Notice when your own experience doesn't match someone else's, and avoid assuming your path generalizes. Connect people to culturally specific resources when they want them.

## First meetings and building rapport
tags: engagement, rapport, first session, introduction, trust, outreach
ask: What do I say when I first meet someone? | How do I engage someone who doesn't want to talk?
Start by introducing your role clearly: "I'm a peer support specialist. That means I have my own experience with recovery, and I'm here to support you — not to evaluate you." Explain confidentiality and its limits. Ask what they'd find helpful and what they'd like to be called.

Let the person set the pace. Some days engagement is a short, friendly check-in, sitting together, sharing a coffee or simply being reliable — showing up when you said you would. Notice interests and strengths. With someone guarded, low-pressure contact and consistency build trust over time. Avoid starting with forms or problems. A good first meeting ends with something small and concrete: a next time to meet, one question they want answered, or one thing they want to try.

## Transitions and post-discharge support
tags: discharge, transition, post-discharge, follow-up, aftercare, bridge
ask: Why is the time after discharge so risky? | What should a discharge plan include from a peer's view?
The days and weeks after leaving inpatient or residential care are a period of higher risk for crisis, suicide, return to use and overdose — tolerance can drop quickly after time without substances. Peers can bridge this gap by building the relationship before discharge and following up soon after.

Helpful pieces of a transition: a confirmed first appointment (ideally within days), a plan for medications and how to get them, a safe place to stay, transportation, crisis contacts including 988, a list of supports and meetings, naloxone if there's opioid risk, and a plan for the first difficult evening. A warm handoff — introducing the person to the next provider or community peer — works better than a phone number on paper. Follow up as agreed and document each contact.

## Goal setting that sticks
tags: goals, SMART goals, action planning, recovery planning, steps
ask: How do I turn a big goal into a first step? | What if the goal seems unrealistic to me?
Recovery goals belong to the person and should be written in their words. Big goals ("get my life back") are valuable as direction; break them into small, specific steps that can be done soon. SMART is a handy check: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound — "call the housing office Tuesday morning" rather than "work on housing."

Ask what might get in the way and plan for it. Identify who can help. Start with something likely to succeed to build momentum. If a goal seems unrealistic, explore it with curiosity rather than discouraging it; often there's a meaningful first step. Review goals regularly, celebrate progress, and treat setbacks as information. BlackLab's participant page tracks goals by life area with checkable steps and target dates.

## Supervision for peer workers
tags: supervision, peer supervision, supervisor, reflective practice, ethics consultation
ask: What should I bring to supervision? | What's the difference between administrative and peer supervision?
Supervision supports your growth, ethics and wellbeing. Administrative supervision covers schedules, policy and productivity; clinical or practice supervision covers how you work with people; peer supervision — ideally from someone who understands the peer role — focuses on staying true to peer values. Many certifications require a set number of supervised hours.

Bring specific situations: a boundary question, a disclosure you're unsure about, a hard conversation, a team conflict, a reaction you had. Bring wins too. Ask for feedback on documentation. Keep a simple log of date, length, supervisor and topics — BlackLab's supervision log totals hours by type and flags which count toward your credential. Good supervision is collaborative and safe; if it isn't, it's okay to ask for what you need.

## Peer certification and continuing education
tags: certification, credential, CPSS, CPRS, NCPS, CEUs, renewal, ethics hours
ask: How do I keep my certification current? | What counts as continuing education?
Peer certification is usually granted by a state certification board or a state behavioral health agency, and requirements vary: a training curriculum, supervised work or volunteer hours, an exam, a code of ethics and a renewal cycle with continuing education (often including ethics hours). National options include NAADAC's National Certified Peer Recovery Support Specialist credential, and some states use IC&RC's peer recovery exam.

Keep copies of certificates and training records, and track CEUs as you earn them rather than at renewal time. Good CEU topics for peers include ethics, trauma-informed care, motivational interviewing, suicide prevention, cultural humility, harm reduction and WRAP facilitation.
