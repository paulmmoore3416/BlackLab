## The MHT role and scope
tags: MHT, mental health technician, role, scope, behavioral health technician, psych tech
ask: What's inside an MHT's scope? | What should I always hand to the nurse?
A Mental Health Technician (also called behavioral health technician or psych tech) provides direct support and safety on a behavioral health unit under the direction of nurses and the treatment team. Typical duties: observation rounds and 1:1s, milieu management, helping with activities of daily living, running or co-leading groups, delegated vital signs and fingersticks, meal and fluid monitoring, belongings and room searches, admissions and discharge support, de-escalation, and documentation.

MHTs don't diagnose, assess risk clinically, give or adjust medications, or make treatment decisions. Report to the nurse promptly: any safety concern, a new or worsening symptom, abnormal vital signs, possible medication side effects, a fall or injury, a refusal of care, withdrawal signs, and anything a patient says about suicide, self-harm or harming others. When unsure, report it — it's always within scope to tell the nurse.

## Observation levels and rounds
tags: observation, rounds, Q15, Q30, safety checks, rounding, observation levels
ask: What am I actually checking on a Q15 round? | How do I check someone who's sleeping?
Observation levels set how often staff see each patient. Routine rounds may be every 15, 30 or 60 minutes; higher levels include line of sight and one-to-one. Facilities often stagger Q15 checks so they aren't predictable. Each check means actually seeing the person — not their door or a shape under a blanket — and noting location and behavior on the observation record at the time of the check, never in advance or from memory.

For someone sleeping, confirm breathing: watch the chest rise or listen; turn on a low light if needed. Look for anything that doesn't fit — hands under the blanket, items in the room, a person in the bathroom too long, the door blocked. If you can't find someone, act immediately per policy (tell the charge nurse, search, call the code). Missed or late checks should be reported, not back-filled.

## Line of sight and one-to-one observation
tags: one to one, 1:1, line of sight, constant observation, arm's length, sitter
ask: How close do I stay on a 1:1? | Can I hand off a 1:1 for a break?
Line of sight means the patient is continuously visible to a staff member. One-to-one (1:1) means one staff member assigned only to that patient, within eyesight or within arm's length as ordered — arm's length is used for the highest risk, such as imminent self-harm. The assignment continues in the bathroom, shower and while the patient sleeps, with privacy managed by policy (often the door ajar and same-gender staff when possible).

A 1:1 staff member doesn't take other assignments, leave the patient, or get absorbed in a phone. Hand off only face-to-face to another staff member who acknowledges the assignment. Keep the environment clear of hazards, document at the required interval, and use the time therapeutically — quiet conversation, presence and support. Report any change in behavior or mood right away.

## Milieu management
tags: milieu, unit culture, milieu therapy, community, unit management, structure
ask: How do I tell when the milieu is heating up? | What helps settle a tense unit?
The milieu is the whole unit environment — people, routines, physical space and emotional tone — and it's a treatment tool in itself. A safe milieu is predictable, structured and respectful, with clear expectations, enough activity and visible, engaged staff.

Watch the unit's temperature: rising noise, clustering, a new admission stirring others, conflicts over the TV or phone, someone isolating, or staff tied up at the station. Interventions include moving staff presence into common areas, offering activities, reducing stimulation, separating peers in conflict early, giving individual attention to someone escalating, and keeping the schedule on time. Shift change, meals, medication times and visiting hours are common flashpoints, so plan coverage for them.

## Therapeutic communication
tags: therapeutic communication, communication, listening, rapport, validation, questions
ask: What do I say when a patient asks if I think they're crazy? | How do I avoid giving advice?
Therapeutic communication helps patients feel heard and supports their treatment. Useful techniques: active listening; open-ended questions ("What's on your mind?"); reflecting ("You sound frustrated"); clarifying ("Help me understand what you mean"); validating feelings without agreeing to unsafe behavior; offering self ("I'll sit with you for a while"); giving information simply; and silence, which gives time to think.

Avoid false reassurance ("Everything will be fine"), giving advice or opinions about treatment, "why" questions that feel accusatory, minimizing feelings, arguing, joking at a patient's expense and changing the subject when it gets uncomfortable. Use the person's preferred name, sit at eye level when safe, and keep your tone calm. If you can't answer something, say so and connect them with someone who can.

## Verbal de-escalation fundamentals
tags: de-escalation, verbal de-escalation, agitation, aggression, calming, safety
ask: Where should I stand when someone's escalating? | What's the first thing I say?
Verbal de-escalation aims to help an agitated person regain control without force. Start with yourself: take a breath, lower your voice, slow down. Respect personal space — a leg's length or more, standing at an angle, hands visible and relaxed, and never between the person and the exit or with yourself cornered.

Use one primary speaker. Introduce yourself, use short sentences, listen for the need under the anger, and reflect it ("It sounds like you're worried you won't get your phone call"). Agree with what you can, offer real choices, and set clear, respectful limits with consequences you can enforce. Remove the audience if peers are watching. Call for support early — a show of calm staff presence can help — and know when to tag out to a colleague the person trusts.

## The escalation cycle
tags: crisis cycle, escalation, phases, assault cycle, de-escalation stages, recovery phase
ask: Why does someone re-escalate after they calm down? | What should I do in each phase?
Escalation tends to follow a cycle. A trigger or rising anxiety is followed by escalation (raised voice, pacing, challenging), then crisis (loss of control, possible aggression toward self, others or property), then recovery (energy drops, the person may be tearful or exhausted), and finally a return toward baseline. During recovery the person is still sensitive and can re-escalate quickly if pushed.

Match your response to the phase: support and problem-solve early, give clear direction and space as it rises, prioritize safety and team response during crisis, then give time, comfort and quiet in recovery before any discussion. Debrief when the person is back to baseline. Training programs such as CPI, Mandt and Safety-Care teach their own versions; follow the one your facility uses.

## Restraint and seclusion rules
tags: restraint, seclusion, CMS, face-to-face, monitoring, least restrictive, physical hold
ask: How long can a restraint order last? | What has to happen within one hour?
Restraint and seclusion are emergency safety interventions used only when there is imminent risk of physical harm and less restrictive measures have failed or aren't possible — never for punishment, convenience or staff coercion. In hospitals that participate in Medicare, federal rules require an order from a physician or other authorized practitioner; for violent or self-destructive behavior, orders are time-limited (up to four hours for adults 18 and older, two hours for ages 9 to 17 and one hour for children under 9) and must be renewed in person or by order within those limits.

A face-to-face evaluation by a physician, licensed practitioner or trained RN or PA is required within one hour of initiation. The patient must be continuously monitored, released as soon as it's safe, and every episode documented. Staff must be trained and competent. Your facility's policy and state rules add details — know them before you need them.

## Restraint safety and warning signs
tags: restraint safety, positional asphyxia, prone restraint, breathing, medical emergency
ask: What signs during a hold mean we need to stop? | Why is prone restraint so dangerous?
Physical holds and restraints can cause serious injury or death, most often from restricted breathing (positional or restraint asphyxia), cardiac events, aspiration or trauma. Risk rises with prone (face-down) positioning, pressure on the chest, back or neck, obesity, intoxication, recent exertion and certain medications.

Warning signs: the person says they can't breathe, gasping or noisy breathing, change in skin color (gray, blue or very pale), sudden quiet or limpness, vomiting, or loss of responsiveness. Release pressure and reposition immediately, call for medical help, and follow your emergency procedure. Never put weight on someone's torso or neck. One staff member should be assigned to watch the head, airway and breathing throughout. Only use techniques you've been trained in.

## Suicide risk: warning signs and reporting
tags: suicide, suicide precautions, warning signs, risk, reporting, observation
ask: What should I report right away? | What do I say if a patient tells me they want to die?
Report immediately to the nurse: any talk of suicide, death or being a burden; asking about ways to die; giving away belongings or saying goodbyes; hoarding medications or objects; sudden calm after severe depression; new agitation, insomnia or hopelessness; and attempts to get into restricted areas. Bad news, anniversaries, discharge planning and the first days after admission are higher-risk times.

If a patient tells you they're thinking about suicide, stay with them, stay calm, listen and thank them for telling you. You can ask directly whether they're thinking of hurting themselves — it doesn't increase risk. Don't leave them alone or promise to keep it secret. Tell the nurse right away; the clinical team assesses risk and sets precautions and observation level. Keep the environment safe and document what was said in their words.

## Responding to self-harm
tags: self-harm, self-injury, cutting, first aid, response, non-suicidal self-injury
ask: How should I react when I find someone has self-harmed? | What do I document?
When you find someone has self-harmed, stay calm and non-judgmental. Call for help, ensure safety (remove the object if it's safe to do so), and provide or get first aid per your training. Notify the nurse immediately for assessment. Avoid shock, anger, lecturing or showing disgust — shame can increase distress.

Self-harm can be a way of coping with overwhelming emotions and isn't always a suicide attempt, but every instance needs clinical evaluation. Afterward, search for other items per policy, check the environment for what was used and how it was obtained, and report environmental factors so they can be fixed. Document objectively: the time, what you observed, what the person said, the care given and who was notified. Debrief with your team; these moments affect staff too.

## Elopement prevention
tags: elopement, AWOL, exit seeking, absconding, missing patient, flight risk
ask: What are the warning signs someone might run? | What do I do if a patient is missing?
Elopement risk rises with involuntary status, recent admission, wanting to leave against advice, withdrawal or cravings, family or legal stress, court dates, confusion or dementia, and past elopement. Warning signs: hovering near doors, watching staff codes or keys, asking about schedules and exits, wearing shoes and coats indoors, packing belongings and sudden interest in outdoor time.

Prevention: know who's at risk, report exit-seeking, keep doors and sally ports controlled, never prop doors, check doors behind you, and be aware during transports, visits and outdoor time. If someone is missing, act immediately: notify the charge nurse, call the facility's code, search the unit systematically, and give a description (clothing, last seen location and time). Every minute matters.

## Contraband and searches
tags: contraband, searches, room search, belongings, safety, skin check, prohibited items
ask: What counts as contraband? | How do I search respectfully?
Contraband includes items that could be used for harm or that undermine treatment: sharps, glass, cords, belts, shoelaces, plastic bags, lighters, medications or substances, aerosol cans, weapons and items your unit restricts. Common entry points are admissions, visitors, mail, outdoor time and items returned from passes.

Search per policy — usually with two staff, same-gender for personal searches, and the patient present for room searches when possible. Explain what you're doing and why, be respectful and thorough, and return or store belongings with an inventory. Report findings to the nurse and document what was found and where. Use contraband findings to improve safety: note how items got in and pass that on so procedures or the environment can be changed.

## Vital signs and when to report
tags: vital signs, blood pressure, pulse, temperature, respiration, oxygen saturation, report
ask: What vital sign values should I report? | How do I get an accurate blood pressure?
Typical adult resting ranges: blood pressure around 90–120 systolic and 60–80 diastolic; pulse 60–100 beats per minute; respirations 12–20 breaths per minute; oral temperature about 97–99°F (36.1–37.2°C); oxygen saturation 95–100% on room air. Your facility's policy sets the exact values that must be reported — follow it.

Report promptly any value outside the parameters, any sudden change from the person's baseline, and symptoms such as chest pain, shortness of breath, dizziness, confusion, fever, or a fall. Get accurate readings: the right cuff size, the arm supported at heart level, the person seated and rested, legs uncrossed. Re-check unexpected values, and if a patient looks unwell, tell the nurse even if the numbers look normal.

## Medication side effects to watch for and report
tags: side effects, EPS, akathisia, NMS, lithium toxicity, serotonin syndrome, clozapine
ask: What does akathisia look like? | Which side effects are emergencies?
MHTs don't manage medications, but they're often first to see side effects. Report to the nurse: stiffness, tremor, shuffling gait or drooling (extrapyramidal symptoms); inner restlessness and constant pacing (akathisia); sudden muscle spasms of the neck, eyes or jaw (acute dystonia — urgent); repetitive mouth or tongue movements; dizziness on standing; heavy sedation; constipation; and rapid weight change.

Some reactions are emergencies — tell the nurse immediately: high fever with rigid muscles and confusion (possible neuroleptic malignant syndrome); agitation, sweating, fever, tremor and diarrhea (possible serotonin syndrome); vomiting, diarrhea, coarse tremor, unsteadiness or confusion in someone taking lithium (possible toxicity); and fever, sore throat or signs of infection in someone taking clozapine. Also report rash, swelling or trouble breathing.

## Alcohol, benzodiazepine and opioid withdrawal
tags: withdrawal, alcohol withdrawal, benzodiazepine, opioid withdrawal, detox, seizures, DTs
ask: Which withdrawal signs are dangerous? | What do opioid withdrawal symptoms look like?
Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal can be life-threatening. Signs include tremor, sweating, anxiety, agitation, rapid heart rate, high blood pressure, nausea, insomnia, and hallucinations; severe withdrawal can cause seizures and delirium tremens (confusion, fever, severe agitation), often 48 to 96 hours after the last drink. Nurses use scales such as CIWA-Ar; MHTs report changes, take delegated vitals on schedule, and maintain seizure and fall precautions.

Opioid withdrawal is extremely uncomfortable though rarely life-threatening on its own: yawning, runny nose and eyes, goosebumps, sweating, dilated pupils, muscle aches, abdominal cramps, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, anxiety and cravings. Report symptoms so the nurse can treat them. Lost tolerance after withdrawal means a much higher overdose risk if someone uses again after leaving.

## Recognizing overdose and using naloxone
tags: overdose, naloxone, Narcan, opioid overdose, emergency, rescue breathing
ask: What are the signs of an opioid overdose? | How does naloxone work?
Signs of opioid overdose: unresponsive or can't be woken, slow, shallow or stopped breathing, gurgling or snoring sounds, pinpoint pupils, and blue or gray lips and fingertips. Call for help and activate your emergency response (a medical code or 911), give naloxone, support breathing and stay with the person.

Naloxone (for example the nasal spray) reverses opioid effects for 30 to 90 minutes. It's safe and does nothing harmful if opioids aren't present. A second dose may be needed after two to three minutes if there's no response, especially with fentanyl. Because naloxone can wear off before the opioid, the person needs medical evaluation even after waking. Withdrawal symptoms can appear suddenly when they wake up — stay calm and reassuring. Know where naloxone is kept on your unit.

## Common conditions in plain language
tags: diagnoses, depression, bipolar, schizophrenia, PTSD, anxiety, personality disorder
ask: What does mania look like on the unit? | How should I think about a diagnosis as a tech?
Knowing common conditions helps you recognize changes to report, not diagnose. Major depression: persistent low mood, loss of interest, sleep and appetite changes, hopelessness. Bipolar disorder: episodes of depression and mania — high energy, little sleep, rapid speech, grandiosity and impulsivity. Schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders: hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thinking and withdrawal. PTSD: intrusive memories, nightmares, hypervigilance, avoidance and startle. Anxiety disorders: excessive worry, panic attacks. Borderline personality disorder: intense emotions, fear of abandonment, impulsivity and self-harm. Substance use disorders often co-occur.

A diagnosis describes symptoms, not the person. Behavior on the unit usually makes sense as a response to symptoms, fear or unmet needs. Observe and report what you see and hear, in objective terms.

## Communicating with someone experiencing psychosis
tags: psychosis, hallucinations, delusions, responding to internal stimuli, paranoia
ask: Should I argue with a delusion? | What do I say when someone hears voices?
When someone is experiencing psychosis, keep communication simple, calm and concrete. Use short sentences, one idea at a time, and a quiet environment. Don't argue with delusions or pretend to agree with them; acknowledge the feeling instead ("That sounds frightening") and focus on what's real and needed now ("I'm here, and you're safe on this unit").

If someone appears to be responding to voices, you can ask gently what they're hearing and whether the voices are telling them to do anything — and report command hallucinations to the nurse right away. Paranoid patients may fear touch, food or medications; explain what you're doing before you do it and give space. Note the content and timing of what you observe for the team.

## Communicating with someone in mania
tags: mania, bipolar, hypomania, overstimulation, impulsivity, sleep
ask: How do I redirect someone who won't stop talking? | Why does someone in mania get irritable?
People in mania may be energetic, talkative, distractible, irritable or grandiose, and may sleep very little. Overstimulation makes it worse. Lower the stimulation — a quieter space, fewer people, dim lighting — and keep your own tone calm and even. Use brief, clear statements and set gentle, consistent limits on intrusive behavior.

Redirect toward simple, contained activities, and support rest, food and fluids, since people in mania may forget to eat or drink. Avoid arguing with grandiose ideas or getting drawn into debates. Irritability can escalate quickly when someone feels blocked, so offer choices and give them time. Watch boundaries with peers, including impulsive sexual behavior and giving away belongings, and report sleep and behavior patterns to the nurse.

## Working with adolescents
tags: adolescents, youth, teens, child and adolescent, developmentally appropriate, boundaries
ask: What's different about working on an adolescent unit? | How do I set limits with teens without power struggles?
Adolescent units need structure, consistency and genuine relationships. Teens test limits and are highly sensitive to fairness, respect and being heard. Explain the reasons for rules, enforce them consistently across staff, and avoid public power struggles — address issues privately when you can. Offer choices within limits and praise specific positive behavior.

Expect peer dynamics: alliances, conflict, romantic interest and contagion of behaviors. Supervise closely during transitions and unstructured time. Keep professional boundaries — you're a supportive adult, not a friend; avoid personal disclosure and never share social media. Families or guardians are usually involved, and reporting requirements for abuse or neglect apply. Watch for bullying, self-harm and boundary violations, and report them.

## Older adults, dementia and delirium
tags: geriatric, older adults, dementia, delirium, falls, sundowning, geropsych
ask: How can I tell delirium from dementia? | What helps with sundowning?
On geriatric units, safety and dignity go together. Dementia is a gradual, progressive decline in memory and thinking. Delirium is a sudden change in attention and awareness, often fluctuating over the day, and is a medical emergency often caused by infection, medications, dehydration or pain. Report any sudden change in confusion, alertness or behavior right away.

Help with orientation (clocks, calendars, natural light), hearing aids and glasses, and a predictable routine. For late-day agitation (sundowning), reduce noise, keep lighting steady, offer calm activities and check for unmet needs like toileting, hunger, pain or fatigue. Fall prevention is critical: non-slip footwear, call lights within reach, clear paths and assistance with walking. Monitor intake and output, skin integrity and swallowing safety.

## Trauma-informed care on the unit
tags: trauma-informed care, trauma, unit practice, triggers, re-traumatization, choice
ask: What are common trauma triggers on an inpatient unit? | How do I make searches and holds less traumatizing?
Many patients have trauma histories, and hospital routines — locked doors, searches, being watched, physical holds, loss of control — can be re-traumatizing. Trauma-informed care asks staff to prioritize safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration and empowerment in everyday interactions.

Practical steps: explain what you're doing before you do it; knock and announce yourself; offer choices wherever possible; respect privacy and modesty; ask about triggers and calming preferences at admission and share them with the team; use comfort rooms and sensory tools; and avoid restrictive interventions whenever possible. After any incident, help the person regain a sense of control. Staff trauma matters too — debriefing and support after incidents are part of a trauma-informed unit.

## Documentation principles for MHTs
tags: documentation, charting, objective, late entry, observation record, legal record
ask: What makes a note objective? | How do I correct a mistake in the chart?
The medical record is a legal document. Write what you saw and heard, not opinions or labels: "Patient paced the hallway for 20 minutes, said 'I need to get out of here,' and accepted an offer to walk to the courtyard with staff" rather than "Patient was agitated and manipulative." Quote patients' own words. Include times, interventions offered and the response.

Document promptly and only for care you provided or observed. Never document in advance, back-fill rounds you didn't do, or alter entries. Use your facility's process for corrections and late entries. Avoid abbreviations your facility doesn't allow. Keep personal notes about patients off personal devices unless your facility permits it — BlackLab drafts use initials only and are meant to be copied into the official record.

## SBAR and shift report
tags: SBAR, handoff, shift report, communication, change of shift
ask: What belongs in a good handoff? | How do I report a concern to the nurse with SBAR?
SBAR structures communication: Situation (what's happening now), Background (relevant context), Assessment (what you observed and think is going on — for MHTs, observations rather than clinical judgments), and Recommendation (what you need or suggest). It works for shift report and for calling a nurse about a concern.

At shift change, cover each patient's observation level and precautions, notable behaviors, sleep, intake, groups, incidents, family or legal events and anything pending. Include unit-level items: census, admissions and discharges, room changes, maintenance issues, sharps counts and environmental sweeps. Use a consistent order and give the oncoming staff time to ask questions. BlackLab's SBAR handoff pulls in your census and last checks automatically.

## Environmental safety and ligature risks
tags: ligature, ligature risk, environment of care, Joint Commission, NPSG 15.01.01, anti-ligature
ask: What is a ligature point? | What does the Joint Commission expect for psychiatric units?
A ligature point is anything a cord, sheet or other material could be tied to or looped over to hang or strangle — door hinges, handles, closers, hooks, shower heads, faucets, grab bars, pipes, light fixtures, sprinkler heads, vents, bed frames, and furniture. Hanging is a leading method of inpatient suicide.

The Joint Commission's National Patient Safety Goal on suicide prevention (NPSG 15.01.01) requires an environmental risk assessment to identify features that could be used for suicide, and expects psychiatric units in hospitals to be ligature-resistant, with mitigation for any remaining risks. Typical controls: continuous hinges, ligature-resistant hardware and fixtures, tamper-resistant fasteners, secured ceilings, and staff rounds to find damaged or missing items. Every staff member should report hazards immediately — BlackLab's Safety Rounds turn findings into work orders.

## Unit safety sweeps and room checks
tags: safety sweep, environment check, room check, unit sweep, hazards, maintenance
ask: What should I look for on an environment sweep? | What do I do if I find a broken fixture?
Environment sweeps at the start and end of each shift — and after incidents — catch hazards before they're used. Check patient rooms and bathrooms for damaged fixtures, loose screws or ceiling tiles, holes in walls, exposed wires, broken furniture, missing vent covers, cords, plastic bags, glass, sharps and hoarded medications. Check common areas for movable or throwable items, unsecured art supplies, unlocked doors to staff areas, and unattended carts.

If you find a hazard: make it safe now (remove the item, restrict access to the area, increase observation), report it to the charge nurse, and get a work order in right away with the exact location and problem. Follow up until it's fixed. Patterns — the same fixture failing repeatedly — should go to facilities and the safety committee so the root cause gets addressed.

## Emergency codes and roles
tags: codes, emergency response, code team, behavioral emergency, medical emergency, fire
ask: What's my job when a behavioral code is called? | What should I do first in a medical emergency?
Facilities use codes for medical emergencies, behavioral emergencies, missing patients, fire and security threats; names and colors vary, so learn yours. Know your role before an emergency: who leads, who responds, who stays with the rest of the patients, and where emergency equipment (AED, oxygen, suction, naloxone, first-aid kits) is kept.

In a behavioral emergency, respond calmly, follow the team leader, and avoid crowding — sometimes the best help is managing the milieu and moving other patients away. In a medical emergency, call for help, start basic life support if trained and needed, and bring equipment. For fire, follow RACE (rescue, alarm, contain, extinguish or evacuate) and PASS for extinguishers (pull, aim, squeeze, sweep). Debrief after every code.

## Patient rights
tags: patient rights, rights, grievance, privacy, dignity, informed consent, least restrictive
ask: What rights do patients keep on a locked unit? | What do I do when a patient wants to file a grievance?
Patients keep their rights when they are admitted, including on locked or involuntary units. These generally include being treated with dignity and respect, privacy and confidentiality, freedom from abuse, neglect and harassment, freedom from restraint or seclusion used as coercion, discipline, convenience or retaliation, participation in treatment planning, information about their care, communication with others subject to documented limits, and the right to voice complaints and file grievances without retaliation.

Know how the grievance process works on your unit and help patients use it — that's part of good care, not a threat. Any restriction of rights must follow policy and be documented by the team. If you see something that looks like abuse or neglect, report it through the required channel immediately.

## HIPAA and personal devices
tags: HIPAA, privacy, PHI, personal devices, phones, social media, confidentiality
ask: Can I keep notes about patients on my phone? | What counts as protected health information?
HIPAA protects identifiable health information — names, dates, room numbers combined with identifying details, photos, record numbers and anything else that could identify a patient along with information about their care. Share it only with people who need it for treatment, payment or operations, in private settings.

Never photograph patients or post about work on social media, even without names. Don't discuss patients in elevators, cafeterias or at home. Most facilities restrict personal devices on units; follow your policy. If you use any personal tool, keep it free of identifiers: BlackLab's Behavioral Health workspace uses initials or room numbers only, encrypts everything on the phone, erases census and rounds automatically and never syncs or backs them up. The facility's EHR remains the official record.

## Staff safety and recovery after incidents
tags: staff safety, injury, workplace violence, debrief, staff wellbeing, post-incident
ask: What should I do if I get hurt at work? | Why do staff debriefs matter?
Behavioral health workers face higher rates of workplace violence than most occupations. Safety comes from training, teamwork, enough staff, good environments and a culture where staff call for help early. Know your duress or panic alarm, stay aware of exits, and never go into a room with an escalating patient alone if policy calls for two.

If you're hurt, get care, report the injury and complete the required documentation promptly — even minor injuries. After an incident, a debrief helps the team learn and helps people process what happened. Check on each other. Reactions like replaying the event, jumpiness, trouble sleeping or dread before a shift are common; if they persist, use your EAP or talk to someone. Taking care of yourself is part of taking care of patients.

## Infection prevention basics
tags: infection prevention, hand hygiene, standard precautions, PPE, cleaning, outbreaks
ask: When do I need to wash my hands? | What PPE do I need for a blood spill?
Hand hygiene is the single most important infection-prevention step: before and after patient contact, after touching blood or body fluids, after removing gloves, after touching the patient environment, and before eating. Use soap and water when hands are visibly soiled or after caring for someone with diarrhea; alcohol rub works for most other situations. On behavioral units, alcohol-based rub dispensers may be restricted or placed out of reach because of ingestion risk.

Standard precautions treat all blood and body fluids as potentially infectious: gloves for contact, plus gowns, masks and eye protection when splashing is possible. Clean spills with approved disinfectants per policy and dispose of sharps and contaminated items properly. Shared spaces and items — group supplies, phones, recreation equipment — need regular cleaning, especially during outbreaks.
